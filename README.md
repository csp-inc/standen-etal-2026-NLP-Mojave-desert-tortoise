## :information_source: About this Project <a name="about"></a>
In today’s world, social media plays a critical role in both science communication and stimulating behavioral change. Indeed, public attitudes towards a species can determine a community’s willingness to support conservation management actions. As such, outreach–which can promote changes in human perceptions and behavior–can play a central role in determining conservation outcomes. Large and increasing volumes of user-generated, open source online content including social media posts and news articles represent a rich resource for studying public perceptions of and engagement with conservation topics. Here we conduct a public sentiment analysis of social media (i.e., Twitter) and news articles related to the Mojave desert tortoise (</i>Gopherus agassizii</i>, hereafter ‘tortoise’) and outreach programs throughout the tortoise's range. Natural Language Processing techniques such as sentiment analysis offers a paradigm shift from traditional approaches to studying human dimensions in conservation and will provide us with a quantitative understanding of public awareness, attitudes and engagement towards the tortoise, and TLD outreach programs.

## :computer: Analysis
### Step 1: Compile relevant social media posts
We successfully collected Twitter/X (hereafter ‘Twitter’) data using the Twitter API (Roesslein 2020). Our process involved authenticating with the Twitter API and systematically retrieving tweets based on predefined keywords with dates ranging from March 2006 to August 2024. At a minimum, data included tweet text, unique user id, and date. Search queries excluded retweets and non-English tweets. Specific keywords used were “Mojave desert tortoise”, “Gopherus agassizii”, “desert tortoise”, and related variations. We initially pulled 45,214 tweets. Following data filtering and cleaning, our final dataset included 19,192 tweets. 

### Step 2: Compile relevant news articles
To gather news media data we leveraged the Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone (GDELT) library (GDELT Project, 2026), which allowed us to scrape all web sources in the GDELT database for article links relevant to the Mojave desert tortoise. If a URL provided by GDELT did not return any information (i.e., the link was broken), we queried the Wayback Machine API (archive.org) for an archived snapshot of that article. We collected the full text of articles posted from 2013 (the year in which daily article data became available from GDELT) through 2024. Specific keywords used for querying articles were “Mojave desert tortoise”, “Gopherus agassizii”, “desert tortoise”, “tortoise”, “Mojave”, “desert”, and related variations. We initially accessed 90,921 articles. Following data filtering and cleaning, our final dataset included 956 articles.

### Step 3: Perform sentiment analysis
To analyze the sentiment of tweets and news articles, we employed a sentiment analysis model called the Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner (VADER) (Hutto and Gilbert 2014). We specifically chose VADER for its effectiveness in analyzing social media content (Fink et al. 2020). The VADER lexicon (i.e., dictionary of words with assigned sentiment scores) consists of 6,338 English words, ASCII emoticons, initialisms, and slang terms with assigned valence scores for sentiment intensity ranging from -4 to +4. VADER can provide a compound score that reflects the overall sentiment of the text and applies “context-awareness” to its sentiment scoring by considering valence shifters (e.g., “not” in the phrase “not good”), punctuation, capitalization, and degree modifiers. Scores which range from -0.05 to 0.05 are considered neutral, while scores above 0.05 or below -0.05 are considered ‘positive’ or ‘negative’, respectively. After calculating the raw VADER score, we categorized the sentiment of each tweet and article as negative (score ≤ −0.05), neutral (−0.05 < score < 0.05), or positive (score ≥ 0.05).

### Step 4: Quantify baseline sentiment
To quantify the baseline sentiment of Twitter posts and news articles regarding the tortoise, we used a pairwise Chi-square test to compare the number of tweets and articles in each sentiment category (positive, neutral, negative). Mann-Kendall trend tests were conducted to assess the presence of weekly trends in sentiment, engagement, and activity across Twitter and news outlets from 2012–2024. We also used Latent Derelict Allocation (LDA, Blei et al. 2003) modeling to identify which narratives about the tortoise were most salient across online discourse. Finally, to identify major occurrences (i.e., non-outreach, tortoise-related events) impacting online discourse through time, we identified weeks that were outliers for sentiment, engagement, or activity and examined content of tweets and articles posted in those weeks.

### Step 5: Assess the impacts of outreach
We also implemented a formal survey to identify outreach events that may have impacted public perception of the tortoise. We then used this survey information to analyze the impacts of outreach on sentiment, engagement, and activity. We used two-sided Monte Carlo permutation tests to assess if sentiment, engagement, or activity on Twitter were different in weeks with outreach events versus weeks without events (Fay and Shaw 2010). To examine how sentiment, engagement, and activity on Twitter evolved before, during, and after outreach events, we visualized pairwise temporal cross-correlations between event occurrences and weekly sentiment, engagement, and activity on Twitter.

To identify which characteristics of outreach programs were most effective at driving behavioral change and online engagement, we employed AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average with eXogenous variables (ARIMAX) models (Pokhrel and Adhikari 2023, Shyan and Singh 2025). ARIMAX models allowed us to assess if outreach events impacted sentiment of tweets in the weeks during which events occurred. We modeled weekly average tweet sentiment using ARIMAX regression in response to categorical event characteristics.
